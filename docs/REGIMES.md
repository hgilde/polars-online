# Regimes: what the detectors find, measured

The five models this batch added -- `deco`, `rcov`, `hmm`, `corrchange` and
`bocpd` -- are all claims about a stream, and a claim needs a stream whose
answer is written down. `po.sim.regimes` produces one; this document is what
the detectors do on it, and on the Monte-Carlo draws whose null is published.

Every number below comes from `scripts/regime_experiments.py`, which is
committed and **not** regenerated by the gate: the size study alone is 2000
replications across three distributions, which does not belong in a test that
has to stay under a second. To reproduce the whole thing (about 35 seconds):

```
uv run python scripts/regime_experiments.py all
```

The seeds are derived with `zlib.crc32`, not `hash()`, so the numbers here
are the numbers you will get.

---

## 0. The short version

- **A regime that lives in the covariance is invisible to a model seeded on
  the rows.** `hmm`'s default seeding is k-means over the rows, and two
  zero-mean states differ only in their second moments, so it splits them by
  *direction* and lands at 56 % accuracy -- chance. Given the covariances to
  start from, the same filter is at 98 %. §1.
- **A prior that is too wide is the failure mode to watch for**, and it is
  the same failure in two models. `hmm`'s `precision_prior` at 1.0 on data
  whose variance is 1.0 halves every correlation and the accuracy collapses
  to 58 %; `bocpd`'s `prior_scale` too large makes every row unsurprising and
  a real break is never found. Set both from the data's scale.
- **`corrchange`'s monitor cannot be matched to its paper's table to a
  decimal, and the reason is the paper's own DGP.** Wied, Krämer and
  Dehling's Tables 1 and 2 are for "i.i.d. bivariate `t_5` innovations",
  which does not say whether the two coordinates share a scale. They do not
  behave alike: at `|rho| = 0.5` the shared-scale reading makes this
  implementation liberal (.075 against their .040) and the independent one
  conservative (.025), and their number is between. On Gaussian pairs it
  sits at the nominal 5 %, which is what the gate pins. §2.
- **The power moves with it, and further.** Size-adjusted, this
  implementation is above WKD's table on Gaussian pairs (.84 against .587 at
  `T = 500`) and below it on either `t_5` (.43 and .47). Heavy tails cost
  this `D̂` more than they cost theirs, which is the open question in §3.
- **The two changepoint detectors are at opposite ends of one trade.** On the
  same streams, `corrchange(window)` finds a correlation break in a median of
  29 rows and lives with 51 flagged rows per 1000 quiet ones; `bocpd` takes
  98 rows and raises 0.68 per 1000. Neither is better. §4.
- **Asynchrony costs almost everything at the finest scale and both remedies
  get most of it back.** A true correlation of 0.8 reads as 0.095 tick by
  tick. Refresh-time sampling recovers 0.54 from a fifth of the ticks; the
  lag inversion on `ew_cov(lags=...)` recovers 0.76 while reading every
  return, which is the one that costs nothing extra to keep. §5.

## 1. Does `hmm` recover the stream that made it?

Two states, at correlation 0.1 and 0.8, alternating under a sticky
transition matrix; four ways to start the filter.

| how it was started                        | mean state accuracy | worst |
|-------------------------------------------|---------------------|-------|
| seeded from the rows (`warm_rows`), learn | 0.559               | 0.507 |
| true covariances, filter only             | 0.979               | 0.966 |
| true covariances, and go on learning      | 0.948               | 0.897 |
| true covariances, `precision_prior` = 1.0 | 0.580               | 0.521 |

8 seeds, 4000 rows each, two states at correlation 0.1 and 0.8

The seeded row is the finding: the default seeding is k-means on the
rows, and rows from two zero-mean states differ only in their
covariance, so it splits them by *direction* instead -- the learned
state means reach 0.65 in absolute value where the truth
is zero. A covariance regime needs the covariances to start from (or a
feature in which the regime is a location shift).

And the last row is `bocpd`'s `prior_scale` lesson again: a ridge of
1.0 added to a covariance whose entries are about 1.0 halves every
correlation, which is the whole of the signal here.

The learned transition matrix is not read back through the public API, so
what is scored here is the state path, which is what a caller reads.

## 2. Is the monitor the size its paper says?

`kind="monitor"` is Wied, Krämer and Dehling's closed-sample constancy test,
and the point of using a published test is that its null is published too.
The rejection rate under the null *is* its size. Their tables are for
"i.i.d. bivariate `t_5` innovations"; the two readings of that phrase are
both here, with Gaussian pairs beside them.

| T    | rho  | WKD Table 1 | t5 shared scale | t5 independent | Gaussian |
|------|------|-------------|-----------------|----------------|----------|
| 500  | -0.5 | 0.040       | 0.075           | 0.028          | 0.048    |
| 500  | 0.0  | 0.035       | 0.025           | 0.046          | 0.045    |
| 500  | 0.5  | 0.041       | 0.076           | 0.025          | 0.054    |
| 1000 | -0.5 | 0.038       | 0.071           | 0.018          | 0.042    |
| 1000 | 0.0  | 0.034       | 0.040           | 0.040          | 0.034    |
| 1000 | 0.5  | 0.039       | 0.081           | 0.021          | 0.051    |

2000 replications each; nominal 0.05, s.e. 0.0049

At `rho = 0` every distribution agrees with the paper. At
`|rho| = 0.5` the two readings of "i.i.d. bivariate t_5" straddle
it -- tail dependence makes the test liberal, independent tails make
it conservative -- and Gaussian pairs land near the nominal 5 %. The
paper's phrase does not say which, so the table cannot be matched to
a decimal; what this implementation can be held to is the level it
asks for, which is what `tests/test_corrchange.py` pins.

The right conclusion is not "the implementation is wrong" and not "the
implementation matches" -- it is that **a published table can only be matched
against the DGP that produced it**, and this one is under-specified at the
precision the comparison needs. What can be held, and is, in
`tests/test_corrchange.py`: at `rho = 0` the paper's own figure, and on
Gaussian pairs the level the test asks for.

## 3. And is it the power its paper says?

| T    | innovations | power | size-adjusted | empirical 95% | WKD Table 2 |
|------|-------------|-------|---------------|---------------|-------------|
| 500  | t5          | 0.525 | 0.428         | 1.498         | 0.587       |
| 500  | t5_indep    | 0.400 | 0.472         | 1.256         |             |
| 500  | normal      | 0.824 | 0.837         | 1.335         |             |
| 1000 | t5          | 0.779 | 0.733         | 1.452         | 0.830       |
| 1000 | t5_indep    | 0.648 | 0.750         | 1.195         |             |
| 1000 | normal      | 0.994 | 0.994         | 1.358         |             |

1000 replications each, nominal 0.05, asymptotic critical value 1.3581.
The power depends on the DGP the same way the size does, and by more:
on Gaussian pairs this implementation is well above WKD's table, on a
tail-dependent t_5 it is below it, and the readings bracket their
figure. The size-adjusted column is the one to read across
distributions, since it takes the null quantile from the same draw.

**The open question.** Under either reading of their DGP this implementation
is *less* powerful than their table once the size is adjusted away (.43 and
.47 against .587 at `T = 500`), while on Gaussian pairs it is well above it.
So heavy tails cost this `D̂` more than they cost theirs. `D̂` is the
delta-method long-run standard deviation of `rho-hat`, built from five raw
moments with a Bartlett long-run covariance at `gamma_T = floor(ln T)`; those
moments are fourth-order, a `t_5` has no finite kurtosis to speak of, and a
noisy `D̂` in the denominator both fattens the tail of `Q` (the liberal size
above) and blurs the shift the alternative makes. That is the hypothesis, it
is not tested here, and the paper's own `D̂` would have to be read in detail
before calling the difference a defect.

## 4. Two changepoint detectors on the same break

| detector           | false alarms / 1000 quiet rows | found the break | median delay | worst delay |
|--------------------|--------------------------------|-----------------|--------------|-------------|
| corrchange(window) | 51.33                          | 20/20           | 29           | 53          |
| bocpd              | 0.68                           | 20/20           | 98           | 351         |

20 seeds; three series, 2000 rows, the break at row 1000, correlation
0.2 -> 0.7. Two windows that slide by one row are almost the same
windows, so the window detector's flags come in runs and its rate is
nearer a fraction of rows above the quantile than a count of events.

The window detector is looking at exactly this and flags it
fast; `bocpd` is not built for it -- it is a full joint model, and this is
the one break its `p_change` cannot see -- but its run length finds it and
dates it, and it does so with a much quieter stream of false alarms.

The two are answering different questions, which is why both are here.
`corrchange(window)` is a *test of one thing* -- has the correlation matrix
moved between two adjacent windows -- and it is looking straight at the
break. `bocpd` is a full joint model of the rows, so it pays for generality:
a correlation change is the one break its `p_change` cannot see (no single
row is surprising; the *sequence* is), and the run-length posterior has to
accumulate the evidence. What it buys is the date and the quiet: it says
which row the current regime began on, and it almost never says it when
nothing has happened.

## 5. The Epps effect, and the two ways out

An asynchronous stream: two series, each ticking a quarter of the time, with
a true correlation of 0.8. Previous-tick sampling at the finest interval is
the naive thing to do, and this is what it costs.

| sampling interval | returns | correlation |
|-------------------|---------|-------------|
| 1                 | 119991  | 0.095       |
| 2                 | 59995   | 0.172       |
| 5                 | 23998   | 0.346       |
| 20                | 5999    | 0.634       |
| 100               | 1199    | 0.752       |
| 500               | 239     | 0.745       |

the truth is 0.8

refresh time: 19157 points (81% of the ticks on the grid kept), correlation 0.543
| L  | inverted correlation |
|----|----------------------|
| 1  | 0.095                |
| 2  | 0.170                |
| 4  | 0.294                |
| 8  | 0.456                |
| 16 | 0.608                |
| 32 | 0.699                |
| 64 | 0.742                |
| 96 | 0.757                |

The lag inversion reads every one of the fine returns and needs no grid,
and `L` is the same trade the sampling interval is: too small and the
cross-covariance is still out at longer lags, too large and the sum is
noise. It is a state a bank already keeps (`ew_cov(lags=...)`), which is
the reason it is here.

## 6. Running them

```
uv run python scripts/regime_experiments.py all
uv run python scripts/regime_experiments.py size power
```

The experiments are `recovery`, `size`, `power`, `delay` and `epps`; `all`
runs them in that order. Nothing downloads and nothing is cached: every
stream is generated from a seed.
